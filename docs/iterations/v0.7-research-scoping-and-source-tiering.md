## Goal

Fix the problem v0.6's testing exposed: **the pipeline was only ever as good as whatever a
general web search happened to return.**

v0.6 fixed a real defect — the search planner had been anchoring queries to its training-era
year instead of today. The fix worked exactly as designed, and the reports got *worse*. Knowing
the current date let the model compare a stale research fact against the calendar and announce
that a compliance deadline had passed, when in reality that deadline had been deferred by 16
months. The v0.5 version of the same error was vaguer, hedged, and therefore less dangerous.

> **Making a system more capable makes its remaining flaws more convincing.** Better reasoning
> over bad input does not produce a better answer; it produces a more persuasive wrong one.

That reframed the work. The date was a symptom; **source quality was the disease.** Two problems
had to be solved together:

1. **Nothing guaranteed coverage.** `plan_searches()` invented 3–5 free-form queries. Whether an
   important regulation got researched at all depended on whether a query happened to catch it —
   in testing, the single most relevant professional standard was absent entirely from some runs
   and present in others, on identical inputs.
2. **Nothing guaranteed source quality.** Every factual error found in testing traced back to a
   low-quality source: a training company's blog quoted as if it were the standards body itself,
   third-party SEO pages standing in for a vendor's own terms, and in one case a video.

## Changes Made

### Session 1 — 2026-08-12: Build, test, and the finding that came out of it

**A new pipeline stage: `scope_analysis()`**

A Claude call that runs *before* any searching and does no searching itself. It maps the
regulatory territory across five fixed dimensions:

| Dimension | The question it answers |
|-----------|------------------------|
| Jurisdiction | Which legal jurisdictions plausibly apply? What is stated vs inferred? |
| Profession | What professional or sector body governs this field? |
| Data | What categories of data does this handle? |
| Activity | What does the AI *do* that regulation cares about? |
| Vendor | Who processes the data, under what terms? |

The questions generalise to any industry; the answers do not. It returns structured JSON rather
than prose — assumptions (each with its basis and what changes if it is wrong), candidate
regulations (each with a status: likely applies / may apply / likely not applicable **with a
checkable reason**, plus one targeted search query), and the authoritative domains for that
specific scenario.

**Two-tier research**

`gather_research()` now runs one targeted search per candidate regulation rather than a handful
of free-form queries, so coverage follows from the dimension list instead of luck. Tier 1
restricts the search to the authoritative domains scoping identified; Tier 2 (unrestricted) runs
only where Tier 1 returned nothing. `search_web()` gained an `include_domains` parameter to
support this. Every source carries its tier through to the analysis and into the report's
source list.

The joint between the two ideas is `official_domains`: "prefer official sources" cannot be a
fixed setting, because the right domains depend entirely on the scenario. Scoping is what
produces them — which is why these are one change, not two.

**The report shows its own work**

Two new sections. **Scope & Assumptions** opens the report: who the analysis assumed you are,
what it inferred rather than knew, and what changes materially if an assumption is wrong.
**Coverage** closes it: what was considered, what was ruled out *with reasoning a reader can
check*, and which authoritative domains were actually searched (supplied by the pipeline, so the
report cannot invent them).

An exclusion is only worth something if it shows its reasoning. "Excluded: HIPAA" is useless —
the reader has to take it on trust. "Excluded: binds covered entities and their business
associates handling protected health information; professional coaching is not healthcare
treatment" is checkable, and can be argued with.

**No fallback on scoping failure**

New `ScopingError`. If scoping fails to produce usable output, the pipeline retries once and
then aborts honestly. Falling back to the v0.6 search planner was considered and rejected: it
would silently ship the exact pipeline that failed its accuracy test, and the user would have no
way to tell which one had run.

**Observability**

The scoping step is its own Langfuse span. Two new trace scores: the number of candidate
regulations and the share of sources that came from Tier 1. `analysis_runs` gained
`scoping_sec`, `num_candidate_regimes`, `pct_tier1_sources`, and `scope_json` — the complete
scoping output per run. That last column exists because the v0.6 analysis of run-to-run
variation had to be done by re-reading report files by hand; storing the scope makes the same
analysis a SQL query.

## Test Results

Three runs on identical inputs for one professional-services scenario, each claim checked
against a hand-verified answer key, plus the `regtech` scenario as a smoke test.

### What the changes fixed

| Finding | v0.6 | v0.7 |
|---------|------|------|
| Governing professional body found and cited to its own domain | Missing entirely in 2 of 6 runs; elsewhere quoted from a training company's blog | Present in 3 of 3, cited to the body's own site |
| Vendor policy claims | Three mutually contradictory retention claims across runs, each stated confidently | Consistent across runs: the terms were not found in research, and the report says so and recommends obtaining them directly |
| Assumptions | Never stated — a run could silently assume a product tier and build its top finding on it | Stated in 3 of 3, with what changes if wrong |
| Exclusions | Regulations were silently absent | Ruled out explicitly with checkable reasoning |

The vendor-facts result is the most significant for trustworthiness. In v0.6 two reports placed
side by side disagreed with each other about basic vendor policy, each with equal confidence.
v0.7 replaced confident fabrication with honest uncertainty — a smaller claim, but a true one.

### What the changes did not fix

**Ranking still drifts.** The *set* of regulations considered is now stable across runs, which
is what the design predicted. But which regulation is ranked most urgent still varies between
runs on identical inputs. Coverage became deterministic; prioritisation did not.

**The tier system leaks.** Two separate causes, both visible in the source lists:

1. The search provider's domain restriction is not strict — when the allowlist matches poorly it
   appears to backfill with out-of-domain results, so commentary can arrive inside a Tier 1
   call and inherit the Tier 1 label.
2. Scoping occasionally lists an official-*looking* commercial site among the authoritative
   domains.

The consequence is not cosmetic. In one run, a stale claim from leaked commentary carried an
official-source tag, so the analysis prompt's rule about flagging commentary-backed claims never
fired — and the report repeated the v0.6 "deadline has passed" error. **Source quality remains
the binding constraint**, and this is the third consecutive version where the most dangerous
error traced to a source rather than to reasoning.

The fix is to verify tier membership in code — check each returned URL against the domain list
and demote anything that does not match — rather than trusting either the search provider or the
model's domain list. That is not yet implemented.

**Timing:** ~165–180s per run, up from ~110s in v0.6. One extra Claude call plus one search per
candidate regulation. The UI copy was updated to set the expectation honestly rather than hide it.

## What This Version Taught

**Making coverage deterministic does not make ranking deterministic.** Both look like
"consistency" problems from the outside, but they have different causes and need different
fixes. Structuring *what gets researched* was a data-flow change; structuring *what matters
most* is a judgement the analysis prompt still makes freely.

**A pipeline that labels its own sources must verify those labels.** A trust signal that can be
wrong is worse than no signal, because downstream logic — and the reader — will believe it. The
tier tag was designed as evidence and shipped as an assertion.

**Honest uncertainty is a feature, not a gap.** The most reassuring result in this round is the
report saying it could not find the vendor's terms and recommending they be obtained directly.
That is less impressive than an answer and considerably more useful than a wrong one.
